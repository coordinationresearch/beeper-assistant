# Playbook

Moves for handling people over text, one section per situation, distilled from books on conversation, friendship, and networking. Each move names its source. Read the section for the situation in front of you and use what fits this Person and this Chat. The messages, the Person's Notes, and how the Owner writes there outrank any move here, and the Owner's Lessons outrank the move they disagree with. Never put a book's name or a move's name in a message.

## Reading how they feel

When: replies get shorter or slower than this person's usual, emoji and punctuation drop away, a bare "k", "fine", or "haha" arrives, the tone turns formal, or the Owner asks "is she mad at me?"

- Read each text against this person's own history. Text drops the nonverbal channel that carries most emotion, so the signal left is a change from their usual length, reply gap, punctuation, and emoji, or words that clash with those cues, like "all good!" after two days of silence. (Goleman, *Emotional Intelligence*; Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Keep the fact apart from the story. "Replied 'ok' after six hours" is a fact and "she's annoyed" is one story, so give the Owner a second plausible reading and don't comb old messages for proof, since any story can find some. (Grenny, *Crucial Conversations*; Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- Treat going quiet and lashing out as the same signal. Dodged questions, "lol" deflections, sarcasm, and "you always" all mean the person no longer feels safe, so step off the topic and restate good intent before going back to it. (Grenny, *Crucial Conversations*)
- Name the feeling in one tentative clause, then move on. "Sounds like this week has been a lot" beats a paragraph of validation, and a wrong guess is cheap because their correction ("no, just tired") tells you the real state. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Echo instead of interrogating. Send their key word or two back as a question ("the whole weekend?") to draw out more without steering, and match their length and energy, so a three-word text gets a short reply. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*; Goleman, *Emotional Intelligence*)
- Look for a second topic inside a reply. If a question about Friday gets back "must be nice to have free time", answer Friday in one line and name the other thing separately ("that sounded like it's about more than Friday") instead of letting it pass. (Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- Notice when a topic has run out. Longer gaps, "haha" with nothing after it, and repeats of what was already said mean change the subject or let the thread rest, not press the point. (Brooks, *Talk*)

Avoid:
- Treating one curt text as a verdict and drafting an apology for an offense nobody named. Strong feelings push toward strong readings, the Owner's included. (Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- Asking "why" ("why are you being short?"), which reads as an accusation and makes people dig in. Ask with "what" or "how". (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*; Grant, *Think Again*)
- Counting a flat "sure" or "sounds good" after a long pause as agreement. Voss separates a counterfeit yes from a committed one, so confirm the specifics ("great, 7 at Rosa's?") before relying on it. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

Example: Dana, usually chatty, answers "ok" six hours later. Instead of "Are you mad at me? You seem off.", send "Seems like today got away from you. Thursday can wait."

## Their good news

When: their message announces a new job, promotion, engagement, launch, acceptance or win, often with caps, "!!" or "guess what", or the Owner asks to congratulate someone.

- Match the register of their message before adding warmth. Caps and three exclamation marks get energy back, while a quiet one-line mention gets a short, warm reply instead of a row of emoji. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*; Haidt, *The Happiness Hypothesis*)
- Name the exact win and the work that got them there. "Four rounds and a take-home, and you got it" beats "so smart" or "so deserved", because praise for effort lands better than praise for talent. (Dweck, *Mindset*)
- Ask one question that lets them tell it again, such as how they found out or which part they are proudest of. Lowndes says to learn what someone is proudest of and later praise exactly that, so keep the answer for future texts. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Keep the spotlight on them. Leave the Owner's own similar win or "same thing happened to me" out of this reply, since Lowndes says to let them enjoy it and find the overlap later. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- If the Owner heard the news from someone else, say who and pass on anything kind that person said ("Sam told me, he was beaming"). Lowndes finds praise carried from a third party more believable than praise given direct, so never invent it. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Brooks says you can't give too many compliments, while Lowndes keeps the big personal compliment to about one in six months so it stays believable. Send the warm line freely and save "you're the most ___ person I know" for a real milestone. (Brooks, *Talk*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Offer a specific way to celebrate with a day attached, or a small treat, instead of "we should celebrate!" Franco counts a card, a gift or a "treat yourself" payment as generosity that pays back far more than it costs. (Franco, *Platonic*)

Avoid:
- A bare "Congrats!!" or "So deserved 🎉" with no detail, which reads as a cliché sent to everyone. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Comparing or self-deprecating ("wish my year looked like this lol"). Their success is not zero sum, so leave envy out. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*)
- Tacking a caveat onto the congratulations ("just don't let them overwork you"). Carnegie's first rule is no criticism, and a caveat reads as one. (Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*)
- Skipping a congratulations because it is days late. Franco finds people overestimate how awkward a warm gesture will be, so send it with a few words of apology at most. (Franco, *Platonic*)

Example: Maya writes "got the design lead job!!" A weak reply is "Congrats!! So deserved." A better one is "MAYA. Four rounds and a take-home, and you got it. How did they tell you?"

## Hard news

When: they mention a death, diagnosis, hospital stay, layoff, breakup, or failed plan, or the Owner asks what to say to someone going through one.

- Open by naming what happened, in their words. Hold advice and silver linings for later, since people take in nothing else until they feel understood. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Keep the reply about as long as their message. Matching someone's energy shows you understood, and a long sympathy essay is one more thing they have to read and answer. (Goleman, *Emotional Intelligence*)
- Ask one open follow-up, then shift. Brooks finds that follow-up questions draw people out, while Kross finds that going over the feelings again and again (co-rumination) leaves people worse, so ask once and then move toward perspective or practical help. (Brooks, *Talk*; Kross, *Chatter*)
- Ask before offering a different view, then widen it. Check first ("want my take, or just need to vent?"), then zoom out in time or normalize it ("this round cut a lot of strong people"). (Kross, *Chatter*)
- Offer only what the Owner named, and make it specific and reply-free. "Leaving soup on your porch Thursday, no need to answer" beats "let me know if you need anything", because support that asks nothing of them helps most. When the Owner named nothing, the reply is warmth alone: invent no offer of your own, even in a draft, since a promise the Owner never made isn't yours to make. (Kross, *Chatter*)
- Check in later without being asked. A short "thinking of you today" a week on, or on the hard date, is welcomed more than people expect, since we overrate how awkward it will feel and underrate how much it is appreciated. (Franco, *Platonic*)
- After a setback like a layoff, point hope at the person, not the event. "You'll land somewhere good" helps where "everything happens for a reason" doesn't, and a friend's steady confidence can work like a placebo. (Kross, *Chatter*)

Avoid:
- Job-search tips, "at least" lines, or any fix in the first reply. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Joining the outrage at the ex or the boss. It feels loyal, but co-rumination keeps people stuck. (Kross, *Chatter*)
- Steering around the subject in later chats for fear of upsetting them. Sensitive questions cost far less than people expect. (Brooks, *Talk*)

Example: Sam texts "got laid off today", and the Owner wants to drop off dinner Thursday. Instead of "Oh no! Everything happens for a reason. Want me to look at your resume?", send "Ugh, Sam, I'm so sorry. That's a gut punch. Dropping off dinner Thursday, no need to reply."

## Keeping a conversation alive

When: a thread is going back and forth and the Owner asks what to say next, the other person's replies are getting shorter, slower or only "haha", or an older thread is worth picking back up.

- Make the next text a follow-up on their last message, ideally on its oddest detail (a place, a name, a strange word), in their own words. Brooks finds follow-ups do the most to get people talking, and Lowndes says to echo their exact terms, so if they said "gig", don't write "job". (Brooks, *Talk*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Send one question per text and pair it with something of the Owner's own, an opinion or a small fact. Lowndes says never to hand someone a bare question with nothing to work with. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Call back to earlier threads. Ask how the thing they mentioned last time turned out, and reuse a running joke from earlier in the Chat, which Lowndes calls tracking and instant history. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Brooks says to find the fun in what is already there, while Lowndes keeps prepared lines ready. In a running thread, find it, for example by treating a small detail from their message as a huge deal, and if it flops, label it in three words and move on. (Brooks, *Talk*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Switch topics when Brooks's signs show up, which in text means slower replies, "haha" or "lol" with nothing else, and the same point coming back. Brooks finds that more frequent topic changes make better conversations. (Brooks, *Talk*)
- Let a thread end on a high instead of fading into "ok" or a last logistics check. Once the plan is set or the joke has landed, send one warm or funny closing line and don't add a new question. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*)

Avoid:
- A chain of questions with nothing of the Owner's in between, which turns a chat into an interview. Brooks says good questions build the conversation together. (Brooks, *Talk*)
- Teasing at their expense, even mild teasing, since a text carries no tone of voice to soften it. Lowndes says never to joke at someone else's cost, and Brooks prefers humor that builds people up. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*; Brooks, *Talk*)
- Answering a two-line text with a paragraph that covers every point. Lowndes says to shape what you send around how the receiver will take it, and in a thread that starts with their length. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)

Example: Leo writes "took a pottery class, made a bowl". A weak reply is "Nice! What else have you been up to?" A better one is "You made a BOWL? I need a photo and a ruling on whether it holds soup."

## Going deeper with a friend

When: a friend's thread has been plans, links or "haha" for weeks, they mention something heavy in passing ("work's fine, dad's back in the hospital lol"), or the Owner asks how to reconnect or show they care.

- Answer the heavy aside before anything else in their message, and invite more with a short "wait, say more?" instead of a fix. Coyle finds the best listeners push a thought back with more energy instead of just absorbing it. (Coyle, *The Culture Code*)
- Swap "how's the new job?" for a question that asks for a feeling or a story, such as the best and worst part so far, what they are most excited about lately, or what has changed for them. (Stevenson and Greenberg, *The 12 Levers*)
- Go first with one small, true admission from the Owner, then see whether one comes back. Coyle calls this the vulnerability loop, and Franco adds that if they answer with logistics, stay at that level. (Coyle, *The Culture Code*; Franco, *Platonic*)
- Brooks finds sensitive questions cost far less than people fear, while Franco says to match the depth the other person offers and not push past it. Ask the hard question once, plainly, and if the answer is short, take that as their level for now. (Brooks, *Talk*; Franco, *Platonic*)
- Say the affection outright and tie it to a moment, like "saw a corgi in a raincoat and thought of you" or "honestly I miss you". Franco finds people overestimate the awkwardness of this and underestimate how much it means. (Franco, *Platonic*)
- When they are struggling, offer one specific thing with a day ("I can bring dinner Wednesday") instead of "let me know if you need anything". Barker ties a friendship's quality to whether support shows up, and Haidt finds hard stretches are when bonds deepen. (Barker, *Plays Well with Others*; Haidt, *The Happiness Hypothesis*)
- Propose a small ritual so contact stops depending on someone remembering, like a call every other Sunday or a standing walk. Barker builds friendship from time and vulnerability, and his own example is a call every two weeks. (Barker, *Plays Well with Others*; Franco, *Platonic*)

Avoid:
- Advice or silver linings before the feeling ("at least they caught it early", "have you tried"). Lowndes says emotions matter more than facts, so echo the feeling first. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- A deep question dropped cold into a thread of logistics. Brooks's pyramid climbs from small talk, to topics only some people share, to talk only these two could have, so mention a shared memory before the big question. (Brooks, *Talk*)
- Topping a small disclosure with a bigger, heavier story. Franco says to share at the other person's level and skip unimportant vulnerabilities. (Franco, *Platonic*)

Example: Jonah writes "work's fine, dad's back in the hospital lol". A weak reply is "Oh no, let me know if you need anything!" A better one is "Wait, he's back in? How's he doing, and how are you doing with it?"

## Someone new

When: a new contact or short Chat after a party, conference, app match or intro ("great meeting you!"), or the Owner asks how to follow up with someone they just met or get closer to an acquaintance.

- Follow up within a day or two and open with a hook from what they talked about, such as the ramen place they swore by or their half marathon. Ferrazzi says to follow up fast with a personal hook, and Carnegie says to talk in terms of the other person's interests. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*)
- Say plainly that the Owner wants to see them again and attach a real plan, like "trivia Thursday, want in?" instead of "we should hang sometime". Franco advises stating outright that you want to be friends. (Franco, *Platonic*)
- Prefer a repeat or group plan over one big one-on-one, such as a weekly run, a standing game night or a friend's party. Franco finds repeated, easy contact makes people like each other more, and Barker puts a friendship at roughly 60 hours together and a close one near 200. (Franco, *Platonic*; Barker, *Plays Well with Others*)
- Give first. Send the article, recommendation or intro they mentioned wanting, since Haidt finds giving first sets reciprocity going and Ferrazzi treats connecting people as the core of a relationship. (Haidt, *The Happiness Hypothesis*; Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Ask for their advice or a recommendation in their area ("you've done Lisbon, what should I not miss?") instead of offering the Owner's. Barker lists asking for advice, not giving it, among the ways to make friends. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*)
- Turn a small moment from the meeting into a callback and use "we" early ("we still owe that karaoke machine an apology"). Lowndes says a tiny first-meeting story can become an inside refrain. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Coyle says to show a weakness early to build trust, while Lowndes says to keep your negatives for close friends. With someone new, a light self-deprecating line works ("sorry I talked about spreadsheets for twenty minutes"), and real trouble waits. (Coyle, *The Culture Code*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)

Avoid:
- Hedged openers like "not sure if you remember me" or "sorry to bother you". Franco finds people consistently underestimate how much others like them. (Franco, *Platonic*)
- Asking for a favor, an intro or a meeting about the Owner's project in the first follow-up. Ferrazzi says to build the relationship before you need it. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Reading one slow reply as a no and dropping it. Franco says not to take rejection personally, so send one more specific invite before letting it go. (Franco, *Platonic*)

Example: Dana writes "great meeting you last night!" A weak reply is "You too! We should grab coffee sometime." A better one is "You too! I tried your #2 ramen spot and you were right. Want to hit #1 Thursday?"

## Reconnecting after a long gap

When: the last message in the Chat is months or years old, or the Owner asks to reach out to, check in with, or reconnect with someone they've lost touch with.

- Open with the specific thing that brought them to mind, like a place, a song, or news about their company, then ask about their life now. The trigger shows the text was written for them alone. (Licht, *On Brand*; Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Mention the gap in a few words or not at all, and write in the old thread's register, nicknames and running jokes included. People overrate how awkward a reach-out is and underrate how glad the other side will be, so assume they still like the Owner. (Franco, *Platonic*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Read the end of the old thread before drafting and pick up its loose end, such as the move they were planning or the job they were interviewing for. Bringing back a detail from last time shows the Owner was listening. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Add one line of the Owner's own news, honest rather than a highlight reel, and let the other person set how deep it goes before sharing more. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Franco, *Platonic*)
- If the reconnect has an ask behind it, name it in the first or second message instead of staging a catch-up and pivoting three texts later. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*)
- When there's a real occasion, like being in their city or going to the same event, propose a specific day instead of "we should catch up sometime". Dormant ties turn back into friendships through time spent together in person. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Franco, *Platonic*)

Avoid:
- A greeting with nothing specific after it, like a bare "Hope you're well!", which reads as if it went to fifty people. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- A catch-up that turns into a pitch a few messages later. It spends the goodwill and makes the earlier warmth look like a setup. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*)
- Formal phrasing like "I hope this message finds you well" with someone the Owner used to text in lowercase. (Cialdini, *Influence*)

Example: To Jordan after two years of silence, instead of "Hey!! So sorry I've been MIA, life got crazy, hope you're doing well!", send "Walked past that terrible karaoke bar today and thought of you. Did you ever finish restoring the boat?"

## Staying in touch without an agenda

When: no open thread, and the agent sees a birthday, new job, launch, move, or post from someone the Owner hasn't messaged in a while, or the Owner asks to keep in touch with someone.

- Send one specific thing with no ask attached, such as their news, a link that fits something they care about, or the moment they crossed the Owner's mind. "Saw this and thought of you" plus the link is a complete text, and people value these far more than senders expect. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Franco, *Platonic*)
- Give where it counts most. A doctor's name, a job lead, or a tip about their kid's school beats any generic gesture, because health, money, and children matter most to people. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Relay praise the Owner heard about them, like "Dana said your talk was the best of the day". A compliment passed along from someone else is more credible than one given directly. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- When congratulating, name the quality behind the news, like "of course you got it, you out-prepare everyone", instead of a bare "congrats!". Praising a trait gives them a reputation to live up to. (Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- When they thank the Owner, answer with something like "anytime, you'd do the same for me" instead of "no worries". Brushing off a favor shrinks it, while naming the two-way tie sets up the next favor in either direction. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Match cadence to the tie, about monthly for the closest ring and two or three times a year for the next, and accept that many ties are best kept at a low dose. Keep a few ties outside the Owner's usual circle warm too, since they bring news a close group can't. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Franco, *Platonic*; Putnam, *Bowling Alone*)
- Carnegie's moves, like remembering details and praising, win liking, but Barker calls them closer to manipulation than friendship, which he says grows from time and vulnerability. So every few pings, turn one into time together, like an invite to something already planned. (Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*; Barker, *Plays Well with Others*)

Avoid:
- Reaching out only when the Owner needs something, so every text becomes a sign an ask is coming. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*)
- Keeping score, such as waiting because the other person owes a text. The people who reach out first and forgive lapses come out ahead. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*)
- A birthday "HBD!" identical to everyone else's. Add one line only the Owner could write. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)

Example: Instead of "Hey stranger, how's everything?", send "Saw your team's launch made the front page. Of course it did, you rewrote that plan three times. Celebrating this weekend?"

## Asking for help

When: the Owner wants a favor, advice, a referral, or an intro for themselves, or says "help me ask X for...".

- Put the whole ask in the first text with the reason attached. A request with a "because" gets far more yeses, and a text waits until the other person has time to answer it. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Say why this person in particular, such as their experience, their role, or something they once said. Pointing to what only they can offer makes the ask both flattering and specific. (Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)
- Size the ask on purpose, because the books split. Barker says start small and never waste a busy person's time, while Ferrazzi says aim high and offer a smaller fallback in the same text so a next step survives a no; with weak ties, default to Barker's small ask. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*; Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Name the imposition in a few words, like "I know this is out of the blue", and close with a real out, like "no stress if the timing's bad". Voicing their doubt before they do reads as honest, and an explicit freedom to say no makes a yes more likely. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- For advice, ask what they did in a specific past situation, or for the one thing they'd change, never what they think of the Owner's idea. Opinions draw polite compliments, while stories about what happened draw facts. (Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*; Grant, *Hidden Potential*)
- If they say no, thank them and retreat to something smaller, or ask who else they'd ask. Treating them as a collaborator often turns a no into a referral. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)

Avoid:
- "Hey, quick question" or "Got a sec?" with the ask held back. It costs them two replies and a moment of worry. (Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)
- Asking right after doing them a favor, or pointing out what they owe. Let a favor sit a while; reciprocity works best unspoken and delayed. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Going quiet after they help. Tell them what came of it, since people invest more in someone who reports back. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*; Licht, *On Brand*)

Example: Instead of "Hey Ana, quick question when you have a sec?", send "Ana, I'm pricing my first freelance job and you're the only person I know who's done it. How did you set your rate with your first client? Totally fine if you're swamped."

## Following up when they went quiet

When: the Owner's last message has sat unanswered for days or weeks, or the Owner asks "should I follow up?" or says "they never replied".

- Assume the text got buried, not ignored, and write a follow-up that stands on its own without mentioning the silence. Cooperating first and forgiving lapses wins over keeping score. (Barker, *Barking Up the Wrong Tree*; Putnam, *Bowling Alone*)
- Bring something new each time, such as a detail, a link, or a narrower question, instead of "just following up". A fresh hook gives them a reason to answer now. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Jiang, *Rejection Proof*)
- Make the second ask easier to answer, never vaguer. Keep the plan by name, such as the hike or the call, not a looser "catch up", and narrow it to something they can answer in a word: two times the Owner can do, a yes or no, or "which day this week?" when the old times have passed. Retreating to a smaller request often gets the yes the first one didn't, while "whenever works" lets it slide again. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- For a stalled plan or decision, ask a question that makes "no" easy to say, like "Should I take this off your list?" People answer faster when saying no feels safe. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- If they already agreed to something, restate their own words plainly, like "you'd mentioned Thursday might work". People want to stay consistent with what they've said. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Jiang says keep going with a new angle, since a no is one person's opinion and enough tries bring a yes, while Fitzpatrick warns against chasing leads that never move. Change the angle once or twice, then close the loop kindly and stop. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*; Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)
- When they finally reply, answer warmly as if no time passed. Letting them save face makes the next reply easier. (Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*; Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)

Avoid:
- "Bumping this!" or "Did you see my last text?" with nothing new in it. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Guilt or sarcasm, like "Guess you're busy" or a lone "?". Criticism makes people defend themselves, not reply. (Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*)
- Several texts in a row before they've answered. Pressure makes people push back. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Telling the Owner the silence is a verdict on them. A non-answer says more about the other person's week than about the Owner. (Jiang, *Rejection Proof*)

Example: To Priya, a week after an unanswered dinner invite, instead of "Did you see my text?? 😅", send "Found a Thursday slot at that ramen place in case weekends are the problem. No pressure if this month's packed."

## Making an introduction

When: the Owner says "introduce", "connect them", or "you two should meet", or someone in a Chat asks "do you know anyone who...".

- Between friends, go straight to the group. When one side is someone the Owner rarely talks to, or a work contact, ask them first in their own Chat, with a real way out like "totally fine to pass". People resist when they feel cornered, and an explicit freedom to say no lowers that resistance. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Get a two-line reason from the person who wants the intro, covering what they're doing, what exactly they want, and why this person in particular, then paste it rather than paraphrasing. The intro should carry their framing forward intact. (Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)
- Give each person one or two lines that praise them specifically and end with a hook, such as "ask her about the food truck she ran in college". Praise from a third person reads as more credible than self-description, and the hook gives two strangers their first message. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*; Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*)
- Name what the two share, such as the same school, the same city, or the same odd hobby. Similarity and a shared identity make people like each other faster. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Suggest intros nobody asked for when one person's need matches another's skill, and favor pairs from different circles. Ties that bridge groups carry news and chances that a close-knit group can't. (Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*; Gladwell, *The Tipping Point*; Putnam, *Bowling Alone*)

Avoid:
- The naked intro, "You two should meet!" with no reason and no hook. (Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*)
- Adding someone the Owner rarely talks to, or a work contact, to a group with a stranger before asking them. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- A full resume for each person. Brief, light bios get people talking; long ones get skimmed. (Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*)

Example: Instead of "Sam meet Lee, Lee meet Sam, you two should chat!", send "Sam, meet Lee, who ran ops for two bakeries and loves talking shop. Lee, Sam opens her first cafe in March, ask her about the 1950s oven she rescued."

## Rallying a group

When: the Owner wants to plan a dinner, party, trip, or game night, or a group Chat keeps saying "we should do something" with no date.

- Open with what the gathering is for and a concrete plan, like "dinner Tuesday at 7 at Luca's to toast Dev's new job". A host who decides is being generous, and a category like "hang out" isn't a purpose. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*)
- Invite a core few one by one with a personal line about why them, then post to the group naming who's in. A personal invite tells people someone will notice if they skip, and seeing who's already in persuades the rest. (Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*; Vogl, *The Art of Community*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- When a group thread stalls, text two or three people directly with a yes-or-no question. In a group, everyone waits for someone else to answer first. (Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Ask "want the details?" before sending a link or a poll, and count only a typed "I'm in" as a yes. Checking interest first avoids a pile of maybes, and active, public commitments are the ones people keep. (Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*; Cialdini, *Influence*)
- Give a few people a small job, like bringing dessert or picking the music. People show up for what they helped make. (Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*; Parker, *The Art of Gathering*; Richardson, *Get Together*)
- Decide on plus-ones before inviting, because the books split. Parker says thoughtful exclusion protects the purpose, while Richardson and Ferrazzi want members bringing new people; keep a small dinner's list closed and a recurring group's open. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*; Richardson, *Get Together*; Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*)
- Remind a week out with some excitement, three days out with who's coming and a hook for each, and the morning of with the address and timing. The next day, send thanks with the group photo and float the next date. (Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*; Richardson, *Get Together*)

Avoid:
- An open "when works for everyone?" poll in a big group with no proposed date. A host who won't decide leaves everyone waiting. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*)
- An invite that opens with parking, address, and timing. Lead with why, and save logistics for the morning-of reminder. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*; Gray, *The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*)
- Fifteen people at a dinner meant for one conversation. A single shared table works up to about twelve, and real storytelling happens around six. (Parker, *The Art of Gathering*)

Example: Instead of "We should all get dinner soon! When's everyone free?", send "Booking a table for 6 at Luca's next Tuesday at 7 to toast Dev's new job. Sam and Ines are in. You in?"

## Tension and repair

When: the Owner missed, forgot, or flaked on something, a comment landed badly, a thread went cold after a sharp exchange, the other person wrote "it's fine" or "whatever", or the Owner asks how to apologize or raise a grievance.

- Apologize in a line or two that names the impact, not the reason. "I left you waiting an hour, I'm sorry" works where "my phone died and traffic was insane" reads as an excuse, because they judge what happened to them, not what the Owner meant. (Goldsmith, *What Got You Here Won't Get You There*; Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- End on a concrete change instead of a request. A specific plan to do better counts for more than the apology, and "can you forgive me?" or "are we ok?" hands them the job of soothing the Owner. (Brooks, *Talk*)
- Own fault in proportion to it. Fisher allows apologizing for the impact without accepting blame, while Brooks finds that dodging responsibility makes things worse, so save "I'm sorry that left you stuck" for shared or unclear fault and own the act plainly when the Owner caused it. (Fisher, *Getting to Yes*; Brooks, *Talk*)
- Say the worst thing they're probably thinking before they do. After a long silence, "you probably figured I'd vanished, and that's fair" disarms the complaint before they have to make it. (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Raise a grievance in four beats. Say what the friendship means, state the facts, offer the Owner's reading as a guess, then ask, ending on something like "am I reading that wrong?" (Franco, *Platonic*; Grenny, *Crucial Conversations*)
- Match the level of the problem. If it's the third cancellation, a message about this one misses it, so name the pattern, and if the same fight keeps coming back, the real topic is the relationship under it. (Grenny, *Crucial Conversations*)
- When the Owner is angry, draft the reply but suggest a pause before sending. Anger takes about 20 minutes to settle, and Goldsmith's rule for speaking while angry is simply not to. (Goleman, *Emotional Intelligence*; Goldsmith, *What Got You Here Won't Get You There*)

Avoid:
- "Sorry, but...", "sorry if you felt that way", or re-arguing who was right inside the repair. Each turns the apology back into a defense. (Goldsmith, *What Got You Here Won't Get You There*)
- Taking all the blame to smooth things over when both sides contributed. Name the Owner's part and leave room for theirs. (Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- Waiting for it to blow over. Raise it with hope rather than let it fester. (Franco, *Platonic*)

Example: Priya's birthday passed without a text. Instead of "So sorry!! Work has been insane and my phone died, hope you're not mad?", send "I missed your birthday and I'm sorry. It's in my calendar now. Lunch on me next week?"

## Saying no or hard feedback

When: the Owner asks to decline an invite, favor, intro, or job offer, to push back on a plan, or to tell a friend something unwelcome, or a draft of theirs hedges until the point is gone.

- Put the no in the first line with one real reason. Extra reasons dilute the strong one and give them something to argue with, and a plainly named priority ("I'm keeping weekends free this month") works better than a vague conflict. (Grant, *Think Again*; Franco, *Platonic*)
- Turn down the request, and offer something else only when the Owner said they can give it. Work out what they're after, such as hands on moving day, and offer exactly the part the Owner named ("can't do Saturday, can bring boxes Friday night"). When the Owner named nothing, send the no with their reason as they gave it and no substitute, since an offer they never made, even "let me know if I can help", takes back part of the no. (Fisher, *Getting to Yes*)
- Be warm and firm in the same message, joined by "and". Thank them for asking, say no once and clearly, and affirm the friendship; "I'd love to, but" makes the warmth sound like a setup. (Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*)
- Critique the act, not the person, with situation, behavior, and impact. Write "at dinner, the jokes about her job made her go quiet" instead of "you're so insensitive"; Scott argues the vague, softened version is the less kind one. (Scott, *Radical Candor*)
- Decide whether unasked-for criticism goes in at all. Scott counts holding back a real problem as ruinous empathy, while Goldsmith lists unprompted negativity and "improving" other people's ideas as habits to drop, so apply Goldsmith's test (is it true, is it worth it, will it help) and say it plainly if it passes. (Scott, *Radical Candor*; Goldsmith, *What Got You Here Won't Get You There*)
- On someone's own life choices, ask before advising, then ask more than tell. Open with "want my honest take?", then use open questions and reflect their words back, because people act on reasons they say themselves. (Kross, *Chatter*; Grant, *Think Again*)
- Say how big it is and leave room to push back. Friends hear a verdict when you meant a tip, so size it ("small thing, for next time") and end on an opening like "tell me if I'm off". (Stone, *Thanks for the Feedback*; Grenny, *Crucial Conversations*)

Avoid:
- Hedging until the point is gone ("maybe, I'll see, not sure yet") when the Owner means no. Scott calls sparing feelings this way ruinous empathy. (Scott, *Radical Candor*)
- Splitting the difference with a half-yes the Owner doesn't want, like "maybe I can come for an hour". (Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)
- Preaching or prosecuting ("you deserve better", "he's using you"). People defend a position harder when it's attacked. (Grant, *Think Again*)

Example: Leo asks for help moving on Saturday. The Owner is at their sister's and offers boxes and pizza Friday night. Instead of "Ahh I'd love to but I have a thing and my back's been weird, so sorry!!", send "Can't do Saturday, I'm at my sister's. Want me to bring boxes and pizza Friday night?"
